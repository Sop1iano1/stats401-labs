Lab 9 — Geospatial Visualization

Comparing 2025 nominal GDP through a geographic choropleth and a GDP cartogram. Both views use the same IMF-based top-50 GDP dataset and are coordinated through country selection.

1. Dataset Description

This lab uses the provided lab9_gdp_2025_top50.csv dataset, which contains the 50 largest economies by 2025 nominal GDP. The dataset includes an ISO-3 geographic identifier, country or economy name, GDP in billions of U.S. dollars, and GDP rank. Countries outside the provided top-50 dataset are treated as missing data, not as zero GDP.

  • 50 economies
  • 2025 nominal GDP
  • Billions of USD as the quantitative variable
  • ISO-3 as the geographic join key

Geographic boundaries are joined to the statistical data using ISO-3 identifiers. The geographic map uses D3's geoNaturalEarth1() projection and geoPath() to draw the GeoJSON boundaries.

4. Design and Comparison

1. Which countries are most visually prominent in each map?

In the choropleth, the United States and China are most visually prominent because they have the darkest colors. In the cartogram, they are also prominent because their much larger GDP values produce substantially larger circles.

2. Which countries become much larger or smaller in the cartogram?

The United States and China become much larger because their GDP is substantially higher than that of most other economies. Geographically large countries such as Canada, Russia, and Australia become relatively smaller because their land area is no longer the primary size encoding.

3. What geographic information does the choropleth preserve?

The choropleth preserves country boundaries, geographic location, relative position, and continental structure. Countries remain recognizable within their actual geographic context.

4. What geographic information does the cartogram distort?

The cartogram distorts geographic area and country shape because visual area is determined by GDP rather than actual land area. Geographic distances and boundaries are therefore less meaningful.

5. What types of questions or tasks can the choropleth best support?

The choropleth is well suited to questions about the geographic distribution of economic activity. It allows viewers to identify where high- and low-GDP economies are located, compare neighboring countries, and recognize regional patterns across continents. Because countries remain in their actual geographic positions and retain recognizable boundaries, the choropleth also supports questions that depend on spatial context, such as whether economies with relatively high GDP are concentrated in particular regions.

6. What types of questions or tasks can the cartogram best support?

The cartogram is better suited to comparing the relative economic magnitude of countries. By encoding GDP through area, it makes large differences in economic size visually prominent and allows viewers to compare countries based on the amount of GDP they represent rather than their physical land area. It is especially useful for identifying the economies that account for a large share of global economic output and for seeing how economic size differs from the apparent size of countries on a conventional map.

7. How does changing from color encoding to area encoding change your perception of global economic size?

Changing from color encoding to area encoding changes the way quantitative differences are perceived. In the choropleth, GDP differences are expressed through color intensity while the familiar geographic shapes and locations remain visible. This makes it easier to connect economic values with geographic regions. In the cartogram, larger GDP values produce larger visual areas, making differences in economic magnitude feel more direct and substantial. At the same time, this encoding reduces the reliability of geographic size and shape as spatial information. The change therefore creates a trade-off: the choropleth provides stronger geographic context, while the cartogram gives greater visual emphasis to relative economic magnitude.
