Lab 8 — Exploring the DKU Undergraduate Bulletin

Two coordinated views of a 400-page bulletin: where a passage sits in the document, and what it means.

1. Corpus Description

Source: Bulletin of Duke Kunshan University Undergraduate Instruction, version 2021–22, published as a 400-page PDF by Duke Kunshan University (official file, accessed September 2026).

The PDF was parsed with PyMuPDF. Each paragraph block became one candidate passage, and the document's own bookmark outline (1,142 entries) was used to recover the chapter, section, and subsection that each block belongs to, along with its page number. Running footers, bare page numbers, the table of contents, and fragments shorter than 25 words were removed.

  • 1,107 raw passages
  • 1,022 after cleaning and de-duplication
  • 84 words average length (median 81, range 25–452)
  • 12 chapters, 81 formal sections

5. Semantic Findings

What are the major semantic topics?

Eight topics emerged. Four are subject areas that come almost entirely from the course catalogue: History, Politics and Global Society (173 passages), Natural Sciences, Math and Computing (146), Environment, Health and Policy (124), China, Language and Culture (93), and Media, Arts and Digital Culture (82). The others are administrative: Grades, Course Load and Academic Standing (165), Credits, Transfer and Study Away (130), and Interdisciplinary Seminars and Student Support (109). The split reflects the bulletin's dual purpose as both a course catalogue and a policy handbook.

Which topics appear across multiple formal sections?

The two administrative topics are the ones that cut across the document. Credits, Transfer and Study Away appears in 11 of the 12 chapters and 53 different sections, and Grades, Course Load and Academic Standing in 8 chapters and 40 sections. The subject topics are the opposite: History, Politics and Global Society and Media, Arts and Digital Culture each occur in a single chapter and essentially two sections. Rules about credit are restated wherever they are relevant, while subject matter stays where it is filed.

Which formal sections are the most semantically diverse?

Measured by the entropy of each section's topic distribution, Course Descriptions is the most diverse (all eight topics, entropy 2.59 bits), followed by Majors (seven topics, 2.48) — expected, since both are containers for every discipline. More informative are the mid-sized policy sections: Degree Requirements (1.57), Estimated Expenses (1.46), and Entrance Credit and Placement (1.41) each mix three topics. At the other extreme, both Academic Warning, Probation, and Suspension sections and the Academic Calendar fall entirely within one topic.

Which passages from different sections are semantically similar?

The strongest cross-section pairs are near-duplicates: the probation rules for the Classes of 2022–2024 and for the Class of 2025 and beyond contain passages with cosine similarity above 0.99 (e.g. p0211 on page 58 and p0223 on page 60). More interesting are the cross-chapter pairs, all about credit: Degree Requirements in Part 3 and Progress toward Degree in Part 6 (0.90); Transfer of Work Taken Elsewhere in Part 6 and the Office of Global Education and Study Away in Part 8 (0.88). The 40-credit transfer cap is stated in both places.

Which passages are unusual relative to their formal section?

Ignoring the catch-all Course Descriptions section, the most atypical passages sit in Majors: the Data Science description on page 119 and the US Studies description on page 207 are each pulled toward a subject cluster far from the average major description. Within Undergraduate Course Load, the passage on medical problems (page 44) is the outlier, because it is about student support rather than credit limits.

Where does "credit" occur?

Searching credit highlights 120 passages, and they do not form one semantic region. Roughly half fall under Grades, Course Load and Academic Standing (57) and half under Credits, Transfer and Study Away (49), with a scattering in the course topics. graduation (29 passages) splits across the same two topics, while registration (9) is concentrated in the first. The word is therefore a poor search handle on its own: it means a grading unit in one region of the map and an external transfer allowance in another.

6. Design Description

Each cleaned passage was encoded with the sentence-transformers model all-MiniLM-L6-v2, producing 384-dimensional normalised vectors. Those vectors were projected to two dimensions with UMAP (n_neighbors=15, min_dist=0.15, cosine metric, random_state=401) for display only. Clustering was run on the full 384-dimensional vectors rather than the projection, using k-means with k = 8 and the same seed. Each cluster was labelled by reading its eight most central passages together with its top TF-IDF terms, so the labels come from the corpus rather than from a predefined scheme. Cosine similarity over the same vectors supplied each passage's five nearest neighbours and an outlier score, defined as the distance from a passage to the centroid of its own formal section.

In the map, position encodes the embedding, colour encodes topic, point size encodes passage length, and an optional ring marks passages that are atypical for their section. UMAP axes carry no independent meaning, so they are left unlabelled; only proximity is interpreted. The matrix answers a question the map cannot: how a topic is distributed over the formal structure. Rows are formal sections, columns are topics, and cell darkness encodes either the count or the row share.

Coordination is what makes the pair useful. Clicking a matrix cell isolates the passages of one section-and-topic combination in the map, which is how the duplicated probation policies became visible. Clicking a point highlights its cell and draws links to its nearest neighbours, which is how passages about the credit transfer cap in three different chapters were found. Search and the two filters act on both views at once, so a query such as credit can be read semantically in the map and structurally in the matrix at the same time.
